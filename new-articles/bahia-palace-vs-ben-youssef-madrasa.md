# Bahia Palace vs Ben Youssef Madrasa: Which to Visit?

Two buildings, both covered floor to ceiling in the same three crafts: hand-cut zellige, carved plaster, painted cedar. Three centuries apart, and a 25-minute walk across the medina from each other.

If you are choosing between Bahia Palace and Ben Youssef Madrasa, the short answer is this: Ben Youssef is the more concentrated experience, a single spectacular courtyard you can absorb in forty minutes. Bahia Palace is the bigger walk, the better story, and the more variable crowd. Most first-time visitors get more out of Bahia. Anyone who has already seen one Moroccan palace and wants the finest surviving craftsmanship in Marrakech should go to Ben Youssef.

The rest of this comparison is about which of those two describes you.

## Bahia Palace vs Ben Youssef Madrasa at a glance

| | Bahia Palace | Ben Youssef Madrasa |
|---|---|---|
| **Built** | Started 1866–67, expanded 1894–1900 | 16th century |
| **Originally** | Private palace and family home | Quranic school and student housing |
| **Location** | Rue Riad Zitoun el Jedid, southern medina | Northern medina, near the souks |
| **Entry (foreign adult)** | 100 MAD | Check the official portal; priced separately |
| **Time needed** | 60–90 minutes | 40–60 minutes |
| **Layout** | Single level, roughly 8,000 m², many courtyards | Compact, two levels, one central courtyard |
| **Best for** | Story, scale, gardens | Concentrated craftsmanship, symmetry |

## What is the actual difference between them?

Bahia Palace was somebody's house. That sounds obvious, but it changes how the building feels. It sprawls. Courtyards open into gardens which open into more courtyards, and the logic of the place is domestic: this wing for the council, that wing for the family, this shaded corner because it stays cool in August. Si Moussa began it in the 1860s and his son Ba Ahmed expanded it enormously between 1894 and 1900, buying up neighbouring houses as he went. That is why the plan wanders. It was never drawn as one design.

Ben Youssef Madrasa was an institution. Students lived there and studied the Quran, and the architecture is built around that single purpose: a central courtyard for gathering, a prayer hall at one end, and small cells upstairs where students slept. Everything points inward and everything is symmetrical.

The practical result is that Bahia rewards wandering and Ben Youssef rewards standing still. You will walk perhaps a kilometre inside Bahia. At Ben Youssef you will find one spot in the courtyard and stay there.

## Which one is better for photography?

Ben Youssef, and it is not especially close.

The central courtyard is a near-perfect square with a shallow reflecting pool, and when the water is still you get the symmetry shot that fills every Marrakech photo feed. The carved cedar, the zellige and the plasterwork all sit in one frame. You can shoot the whole building from three or four positions.

Bahia is harder. The spaces are larger and more broken up, the light is unevenly distributed, and the best details are often above your head on painted ceilings that are difficult to shoot without a wide lens. It is more rewarding once you know where to look, which is exactly what our [Bahia Palace photography guide](/en/blog/bahia-palace-photography-guide) covers, and the [room-by-room walkthrough](/en/blog/bahia-palace-room-by-room-guide) tells you which courtyards hold the light longest.

One honest warning about Ben Youssef: because it is compact and famous for that one shot, the courtyard can become a queue of people waiting to photograph an empty pool. Arrive at opening or accept that someone will be in your frame.

## Crowds: which one fills up first?

Both fill by late morning. They fill differently.

Bahia absorbs people. It is large enough that a hundred visitors spread across the courtyards feel like a moderate crowd, and if a tour group arrives you can usually walk to a different wing and wait them out. The queue is the real problem at Bahia, not the interior, and it forms at the ticket booth rather than inside.

Ben Youssef does not absorb people. One courtyard means everyone is in the same place at the same time, so the difference between 9:15 and 11:00 is dramatic. If you only have the patience to arrive early for one of these, make it Ben Youssef.

Our guide to [visiting Bahia Palace without the crowds](/en/blog/best-time-to-visit-bahia-palace) goes into the hourly pattern in more detail, and the [2026 opening hours page](/en/blog/bahia-palace-opening-hours-2026) has the current schedule.

## How far apart are they, and can you walk it?

Yes, and the walk is part of the point.

Bahia sits in the southern medina on Rue Riad Zitoun el Jedid, about ten minutes on foot from Jemaa el-Fna. Ben Youssef is north of the square, past the souks. Walking from one to the other takes roughly half an hour or so depending on how lost you get, and you will get somewhat lost. The route takes you through the busiest stretch of the souks, which is either a bonus or an ordeal depending on your tolerance for being offered things.

Two practical notes. First, do it in that order: Bahia first thing, then walk north through the souks, then Ben Youssef. Going the other way puts you in the souks at their most crowded. Second, read our [Marrakech safety and scams guide](/en/blog/marrakech-safety-guide) before you make that walk, because the stretch between the two is where the "this street is closed, follow me" routine is most common.

## Price and time: what are you actually spending?

Bahia Palace charges 100 MAD for foreign adults, roughly ten dollars, with children under seven free. Ben Youssef is ticketed separately and its price is set independently, so check the official Ministry of Culture portal at e-services.minculture.gov.ma rather than trusting a number from a blog, including this one.

Budget 60 to 90 minutes inside Bahia. Budget 40 to 60 at Ben Youssef. Add the 30-minute walk and you are looking at three hours for both, which fits comfortably into a morning if you start at opening.

For a fuller breakdown of what entry costs and where people overpay, see the [2026 entrance fee guide](/en/blog/bahia-palace-entrance-fee-2026).

## If you only have time for one

Choose **Bahia Palace** if this is your first Moroccan palace, if you care about the history behind the decoration, if you are travelling with children who need room to move, or if you want gardens. The [story of Ba Ahmed's rise and fall](/en/blog/bahia-palace-history) gives the empty rooms a meaning they lack without it, and the palace has more variety to hold attention across an hour.

Choose **Ben Youssef Madrasa** if you have limited time in the medina, if you have already seen a palace or two and want the single finest room, or if you are primarily there to photograph. Forty minutes at Ben Youssef will give you more images than ninety at Bahia.

Choose **neither, for now** if it is 2pm in July and you have been walking since morning. Both buildings are open to the sky and neither is comfortable in peak afternoon heat.

## Doing both in one day

This works well and does not feel rushed.

- **9:00** Bahia Palace at opening. 75 minutes.
- **10:30** Walk north through Riad Zitoun el Jedid toward the square, then into the souks.
- **11:30** Ben Youssef Madrasa. 45 minutes.
- **12:30** Lunch near the madrasa, then the souks on your way back south.

If you would rather pair Bahia with something closer, [El Badi Palace](/en/blog/bahia-palace-vs-badi-palace-marrakech) is ten minutes away and the [Saadian Tombs can be combined with Bahia in a single morning](/en/blog/bahia-palace-and-saadian-tombs-one-day). For a garden rather than a monument, compare the two in our [Bahia versus Majorelle Garden](/en/blog/bahia-palace-vs-majorelle-garden) piece.

## The honest verdict

Ben Youssef Madrasa is the more beautiful single space in Marrakech. Bahia Palace is the more interesting building to spend time in.

If someone gave you one hour in the medina and asked you to be impressed, Ben Youssef wins. If they gave you a morning and asked you to understand something about how Morocco's ruling class lived at the end of the 19th century, Bahia wins, and it is not a close call. Most visitors have the morning.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is Ben Youssef Madrasa better than Bahia Palace?**
Neither is better; they do different things. Ben Youssef offers the most concentrated craftsmanship in Marrakech in one courtyard. Bahia offers scale, gardens and a stronger historical narrative. First-time visitors usually get more from Bahia, photographers usually get more from Ben Youssef.

**How long does it take to walk between Bahia Palace and Ben Youssef Madrasa?**
Around half an hour on foot through the medina, though it depends heavily on your route and how often you stop. The walk passes through the souks. Taxis cannot take you door to door because most of the route is pedestrian-only lanes.

**Can I visit both in one morning?**
Yes. Start at Bahia at 9:00, spend around 75 minutes, walk north through the souks, and reach Ben Youssef before midday. Both fill up as the morning goes on, so the early start matters more than the order.

**Which one is better with children?**
Bahia Palace. It is single-level, there is space to walk, and the gardens give children somewhere to move. Ben Youssef is compact and quiet, and a restless child is more noticeable there. Our [family visitor guide](/en/blog/bahia-palace-with-kids) covers the practicalities.

**Do I need a guide for either?**
Not strictly. Bahia benefits more from context because the rooms are empty and the story is not written on the walls, so an [audio guide](/en/blog/bahia-palace-audio-guide) or a [guided tour](/en/blog/bahia-palace-guided-tour) adds a lot. Ben Youssef largely explains itself visually.

**Are tickets sold at the door for both?**
Both are ticketed on site, and both are managed under the Ministry of Culture. Prices are set officially, so you should never pay a premium to a third party for standard entry.
